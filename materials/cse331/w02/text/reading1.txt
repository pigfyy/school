        CSE 331 Reading 1

Due Friday, October 2 at 11:59pm (late policy).

Our motto for this course is: Your job is to deliver code that you
know works. When you say you know your code works, what do you
actually know, and how did you come to know it?

The three articles below answer differently. Willison says you know
when you have seen the code do the right thing yourself and have a
test that proves it. Fishman reports on the group that wrote the
space shuttle's software, whose last three versions had one error
each, and finds the reason in their process, not their people. Hoare
asks how software got so reliable when almost none of it was proved
correct, and answers that programmers use the same techniques as
every other kind of engineer.

Your task: Choose one article, read it, and write a reflection.
See the readings page for information on what you need
to submit and how reflections are graded. When you are done, submit on
Gradescope as described in the "Submit" section below.

Articles

Willison 2025, Your job is to deliver code you have proven to work

Willison wrote this about programmers who hand large, untested,
AI-written changes to code review and expect the reviewer to find the
problems. Proving that a change works is the programmer's job, he
says, and it takes two steps, neither optional. First, test it by
hand: get the system into a known state, exercise the change, and
show your reviewers that it did what you meant. Second, write an
automated test that fails if the change is reverted. A coding agent
must prove its changes the same way, and the human stays accountable
for them. About 5 minutes to read
(cached: PDF,
HTML).

Fishman 1996, They Write the Right Stuff

Fishman reports on the 260 people who wrote the software that flew
the space shuttle. Their last three versions, each 420,000 lines
long, had one error each. The group has no superstars, and nothing in
its process depends on any one person. The product is only as good as
the plan for it: about a third of the work happens before any code is
written, and no line changes without a change to the specification.
The coders and the testers are rival teams, each trying to find the
errors first. And a mistake is never just fixed: the group asks what
let it through, fixes the process, and looks for the same mistake
elsewhere. The group's manager says yes, the process stifles
creativity, and Fishman says that is the point: nobody improvises
code that flies a spaceship. About 20 minutes to read
(cached: PDF,
HTML).

Hoare 1996, How Did Software Get So Reliable Without Proof?

Hoare helped invent the logic for proving programs correct. The paper
opens on a prediction from twenty years earlier, that unreliable
programs would cap the size of software. Instead, telephone exchanges
run on tens of millions of lines of code, and almost none of it was
ever proved. Hoare asks why the prediction failed, and finds the
answer in the practices of every other branch of engineering: design
reviews at every stage, testing planned before the code is written,
errors removed from products already in use, and over-engineering.
Testing cannot show the absence of bugs, so his view of a test is the
quality controller's: a test checks the methods that produced the
code, not the code, and a failing test means fixing the process and
not just the product. Proof itself has played a small direct role,
but the theory behind it gave practice structured programming, types,
and information hiding. About an hour to read
(cached: PDF,
HTML).

Extras

These are optional. You may bring one into your reflection if you
connect it to the article you chose.

Cook 1998, How Complex Systems Fail

Cook is a physician who studies safety in hospitals and other
hazardous systems. The essay is eighteen numbered claims about how
such systems fail, and why they usually do not. Such systems are
always partly broken, and keep working because people catch problems
as they come up. A catastrophe needs several small failures at once.
So blaming an accident on one root cause is a mistake. Hindsight
makes the warning signs look plainer than they were. The fixes made
after an accident usually add complexity instead of safety. He never
mentions software. About 10 minutes to read
(cached PDF).

Leveson and Turner 1993, An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents

The Therac-25 was a radiation therapy machine controlled by software.
Between 1985 and 1987 it gave six patients massive
overdoses.1 Leveson, then at UW, and Turner reconstruct the
accidents from public records. They find real bugs, but argue that
blaming the bugs misses the point: most accidents are system
accidents, with many contributing causes, and naming a single cause
is usually a serious mistake. Earlier machines had hardware
interlocks to block unsafe operation; the Therac-25 relied on
software instead. More than once the manufacturer fixed one fault and
declared the machine safe. Among the lessons they draw: there is
always another bug, and a design must stay safe when the software
fails. About two hours to read
(cached PDF).
The Wikipedia article
covers the same events in about 20 minutes
(cached PDF).

Thompson 1984, Reflections on Trusting Trust

Thompson is one of the creators of Unix. In this short lecture he
shows how to hide a back door in a compiler so that it persists even
after it is removed from the compiler's source code. His point is
that reading a program's source cannot tell you everything the
program does. You also have to trust the tools that built it, and the
people who wrote those tools. The examples are in C, and the last few
paragraphs are about hackers in the 1984 news. About 10 minutes to
read, longer if you work through the code
(cached: PDF,
HTML).
