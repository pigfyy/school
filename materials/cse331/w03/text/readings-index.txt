        CSE 331: Readings

We will do four readings this quarter. For each one, you should choose one of the three
article options we present, read it, and write a short reflection. Reflections are due
on a Friday, and we will then discuss the readings the following Monday. 

Schedule

Each reading asks a question about our motto for this course:
Your job is to deliver code that you know works. The three
articles for a reading answer its question differently.

Reading 1: due Friday, October 2; discussed
  Monday, October 5. When you say you know your code works, what do
  you actually know, and how did you come to know it?

Reading 2: due Friday, October 16; discussed
  Monday, October 19. Knowing takes work: saying exactly what a
  method must do, testing it, and reasoning about whether it does
  that. Is that work the job itself, or a tax on the job?

Reading 3: due Friday, October 30; discussed
  Monday, November 2. How much of that knowing happens before the
  first line of code, and how much of it is worth writing down?

Reading 4: due Friday, November 20; discussed Monday, November 23.
  Sooner or later you will change a program you did not write,
  whether a person wrote it or a machine did. What do you need
  besides the code, and can you get it?

Reflection Content

After reading your article, write a reflection with these three parts:

What does the reading argue? Explain the author's main claim
   and how they support it, in your own words. Reconstruct the
   argument, not just the conclusion. Be specific enough that a
   classmate who read a different article could follow. (About 5 sentences.)

What is your reaction? Which parts did you agree with?
   Disagree with? Are you unconvinced or unsure about any parts?
   Connect the article to your own experience as a programmer: a bug
   you chased, a project that got away from you, a test that saved
   you, code someone else wrote. (About 5 sentences.)

What do you want to discuss in class? Bring a question for
   your group on Monday, one your classmates might answer
   differently. Point to what in the reading raises it: something
   left unresolved, or a claim you would challenge. Give a concrete
   case that sharpens the question. Then sketch two answers: yours,
   and one a classmate might give. (About 5 sentences.)

AI use

A reflection is practice at reading an argument and deciding what
you think of it. You only get that practice by doing it, so read
the article and write the reflection yourself. You may ask AI to
explain a term or an idea in the article, but not to summarize the
article or tell you what it argues.

You may use AI for light editing: grammar, spelling, and trimming.
If you do, say so on the submission form. Submitting AI-written
text as your reflection is academic misconduct. The
syllabus has the course's full AI policy.

In-class discussion

We discuss each reading in lecture the Monday after it is due. You
will talk about your article in a small group. Others in your group
may have read a different article, so be ready to explain yours. Be
ready to discuss and debate your thoughts on the topic too.

We will take 15 to 20 minutes of lecture time to discuss the readings
in small groups. After the discussion, you will each write a brief
response on paper: what the discussion changed in your thinking, or
one new thing you learned. It counts as an in-class activity (see
Grading).

Grading

We grade reflections on thoughtful completion. Each of the three
parts is marked Complete, Partial, or No credit. A part is
Complete when it does what its prompt above asks, in your own
thinking. A part is Partial when it is a real attempt that misses
some of what the prompt asks, such as a summary that never reaches
the author's main claim, or a reaction that only restates the
article. If a part is empty, or if we judge that it shows no real
effort, it gets No credit. The "About 5 sentences" in each prompt
is a rough guide: a part that does its job in four sentences or in
seven is Complete.

The late policy applies to reflections as it
does to homework.
